## 2. Why a lighthouse was needed: Alexandria and the island of Pharos

<p align="center">
  <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/CW_QaitbayCitadel_Front.jpg/960px-CW_QaitbayCitadel_Front.jpg" width="700" alt="Citadel of Qaitbay in Alexandria">
  <br><sub><i>The Citadel of Qaitbay, built in the 15th century exactly where the Pharos stood and partly from its stones. Photo: CarstenW, CC BY 3.0 (Wikimedia Commons).</i></sub>
</p>

- **332/331 BC:** Alexander the Great founds Alexandria on a narrow strip of land (an **isthmus**) facing the small island of **Pharos**, on the western edge of the Nile Delta.
- Later the island and the city were linked by a causeway more than 1.2 km long, the **Heptastadion** (“seven stadia”). It split the sea into two harbours: the **Great Harbour** to the east (today's Eastern Harbour) and the harbour of **Eunostos** to the west (the base of the modern port).
- Over time silt widened the causeway until it became dry land. That is why **Pharos is no longer an island**: what remains is the Ras el-Tin headland, and the point where the lighthouse stood has been eaten away by the sea.

**Why such a big lighthouse here?**
🧠 **Reasoning:** the Egyptian coast by the delta is **flat and low**, with no mountains to use as landmarks, and it has shoals and submerged reefs. A ship coming from Greece or Rhodes saw the coast very late. A very tall tower solved two problems at once: **by day** it was a landmark visible from far away and **by night** a light. It was also propaganda, announcing the power of the new Ptolemaic dynasty to everyone who arrived. **Confidence: 0.85.**

A legend says the people of Pharos were **wreckers** and that the lighthouse was also built to put an end to that. **Confidence: 0.3** (it is a legend with no evidence).

> 📜 Fun fact: the island of Pharos already appears in Homer's *Odyssey*, centuries before the lighthouse. From that island comes the word for lighthouse in many languages: **faro** in Spanish and Italian, **far** in Catalan, **phare** in French and **farol** in Portuguese.

## 3. Construction: who, when and how much

### 3.1 When
| Source | Dates given |
|---|---|
| CNRS / Hairy (2025) | 297–283 BC |
| *Archaeology* magazine (Empereur, 1999) | 285–280 BC |
| Wikipedia (English) | Between 284 and 246 BC (infobox); reign of Ptolemy II (280–247 BC) |
| World History Encyclopedia | Around 300–280 BC |

🧠 **Combining them:** all agree that **Ptolemy I started it and Ptolemy II finished it**, early in the 3rd century BC. The work took **12 to 15 years** depending on the source. An honest approximate date is **“around 297–280 BC”**. **Confidence: 0.8.**

### 3.2 The mystery of Sostratus of Cnidus
- **Strabo** (Greek geographer who visited Alexandria in the late 1st century BC) says that **Sostratus of Cnidus** dedicated the lighthouse to the **“Saviour Gods”** in an inscription of metal letters.
- **Pliny the Elder** (1st century AD) says Sostratus was the **architect**. Not all historians accept this: he may have been a wealthy courtier who paid for or sponsored the work.
- **Lucian of Samosata** (2nd century AD) tells an anecdote: Sostratus supposedly carved his own name in the stone, covered it with plaster and wrote the king's name on top. Over the years the plaster would fall off and only his name would remain. Lucian uses it as a moral example, so **it is probably an invented or exaggerated story**.

🧠 **Analysis:** the “Saviour Gods” (*Theoi Sōtēres*) was the cult title of **Ptolemy I and his wife Berenice**, but it could also mean the **Dioscuri**, protectors of sailors. The phrase is ambiguous, either deliberately or because of what we do not know. **Confidence: 0.6.**

### 3.3 The cost
**800 talents of silver.** A talent was about 26 kg, so we are talking about **more than 20 tonnes of silver**: it has been calculated as roughly **a tenth of the treasury** of Ptolemy I when he came to the throne. For comparison, the Parthenon in Athens is estimated to have cost at least **469 talents**. **Confidence: 0.7** (the figure comes from ancient sources; the conversion to tonnes is approximate).

### 3.4 What it was made of
- Pale **limestone** and **sandstone** for most of the tower, and pink **granite** (from Aswan) for big pieces such as the doorway.
- A mineralogical study linked the stone to the **Wadi Hammamat** quarries. ⚠️ Wikipedia places those quarries “to the east of Alexandria”, but Wadi Hammamat is in the Eastern Desert, hundreds of kilometres to the south. **Confidence in the quarry claim: 0.4.**
- The blocks were joined with **metal clamps** and lead. The PHAROS team has found **more than 2,000 clamps**, and Isabelle Hairy thinks this explains how it went up in **about 15 years**: it is faster than working with mortar.

## 4. What it looked like: height, floors, light and statues

### 4.1 Height (a classic problem)
| Source | Height |
|---|---|
| Medieval Arab descriptions | 103–118 m (only a 15% spread) |
| Al-Idrisi (geographer, visited in 1154) | ~162 m |
| Modern historians | 100–140 m |
| PHAROS 3D model (2026) | **103 m** |

🧠 **Reasoning:** no ancient measurement is exact (they used cubits and spans of varying length), and the lighthouse was **repaired and shortened** several times after earthquakes, so each traveller saw a different tower. The safest figure is **“a little over 100 m”**, taller than almost any building in the world until modern times apart from the great pyramids. **Confidence in “over 100 m”: 0.8; in an exact figure: 0.4.**

### 4.2 Inside
- **Three tapering sections:** a square base with a central core, an octagonal section and a cylindrical section.
- According to the Andalusian traveller **Abu al-Haggag al-Balawi**, who measured it in **1166**, inside there was a **ramp** wide enough for **two riders** to pass each other, and dozens of rooms.
- The PHAROS model (2026) proposes a **causeway on 16 arches** leading to a **13 m-high monumental doorway**, and behind it a ramp serving **67 rooms over 9 levels**.

### 4.3 The light
- At night a **fire** was lit at the top. Medieval sources also mention a **mirror** that reflected the sun by day.
- ❓ **What was burned?** There is no direct evidence. Egypt had little timber, so imported wood, oil or dried dung have been suggested. **This is an open hypothesis. Confidence: 0.3.**
- ❌ **Myth:** that the mirror could **set enemy ships on fire** from a distance. There is no evidence and the physics makes it impossible with the technology of the time. **Confidence that it is false: 0.9.**

🧠 **How far could it be seen? (my own calculation)**
Distance to the horizon is d ≈ 3.57 × √h (d in km, h in metres).
- Fire at **100 m**: 3.57 × √100 ≈ **35.7 km**.
- Sailor on deck at **5 m**: 3.57 × √5 ≈ **8 km** more.
- Total ≈ **44 km**, very close to the **47 km** given by Wikipedia. Atmospheric refraction can add a little more.
This is the **geometric maximum**: in haze or fog a fire would be visible much less far. **Confidence: 0.7.**

### 4.4 The statues on top
**Roman coins minted in Alexandria** (2nd century AD) show **four tritons** at the corners and a statue at the top, perhaps **Zeus Soter** (“Saviour”) or **Poseidon**. After the 956 earthquake the statue fell and an **Islamic-style dome** was installed. **Confidence: 0.75.**

## 5. 1,600 years of life: the Pharos in history

| Period | What happened |
|---|---|
| 3rd century BC | Completed. It becomes the symbol of Ptolemaic Alexandria, the city of the Library and the Museum |
| 3rd century BC | According to tradition, the Hebrew Bible is translated into Greek on the island of Pharos (the **Septuagint**). Alexandria's Jews held an annual festival there to commemorate it |
| **48 BC** | **Julius Caesar**, trapped in Alexandria, **seizes the Pharos**, because whoever held it controlled the harbour entrance. That secured his supplies |
| 1st–2nd centuries AD | It appears on coins, mosaics, lamps and sarcophagi all over the Mediterranean |
| **AD 365** | A great earthquake and tsunami originating near Crete hits Alexandria (context: there is no record that it toppled the lighthouse). **Confidence: 0.6** |
| 7th century | Arab conquest of Egypt (641). The lighthouse is still standing and Arab authors describe it with admiration |
| 796 | First earthquake to damage it seriously |
| 951 and **956** | Cracks, and in 956 **the top ~20 m fall** |
| 1154 | Visited by al-Idrisi |
| **1166** | Al-Balawi measures the inside: the fullest description we have |
| 13th century | The Chinese official **Zhao Rugua** describes it in a book about foreign peoples: its fame reached China! |
| **1303** | **The most destructive earthquake**, originating near Crete (280–350 km away), with an estimated intensity of **VIII or more** in Alexandria |
| 1323 | Another earthquake leaves it even worse |
| **1326** | **Ibn Battuta** visits: one face is in ruins but it can still be entered |
| **1349** | Ibn Battuta returns and it is **no longer possible to enter or even climb to the doorway** |
| 1341 | Death of Sultan an-Nasir Muhammad, who had planned a new lighthouse next to it. The project is abandoned |
| **1477–1480** | Mamluk Sultan **Qaitbay** builds a fortress on the lighthouse base using its fallen stones |

### 5.1 Its architectural legacy
- The **Abusir tower** (ancient Taposiris Magna), 48 km south-west of Alexandria, is a tomb about 20 m high with the same three-part shape (square, octagon, cylinder). It looks like a **scale model** of the Pharos built at about the same time.
- The earliest **minarets** of many Egyptian mosques follow the same three-stage design.
- The George Washington Masonic National Memorial in Alexandria (Virginia, USA) is modelled on it.
- The lighthouse is still on the **flag and seal** of the Alexandria Governorate and on the seal of its university.

## 6. The fall: earthquakes, legends and the Citadel of Qaitbay

### 6.1 Why it fell (factor analysis)

| Factor | What happened | Weight | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Earthquakes** | Alexandria is 350 km from the African–Arabian plate boundary, 520 km from the Red Sea rift and about 300 km from the Cretan arc. The quakes of 796, 956, 1303 and 1323 broke it bit by bit | Decisive | 0.9 |
| **Height and weight** | A solid stone tower over 100 m high has a lot of mass at the top: when the ground shakes, the upper part “whips” and breaks first (that is how the top 20 m fell in 956) | High | 0.75 |
| **Ground** | It stood on an island of sand and rock at the water's edge. That kind of ground **amplifies** shaking | Medium (hypothesis) | 0.5 |
| **Sea erosion** | Waves ate away the tip of the island; part of the platform where it stood is now underwater | Medium | 0.7 |
| **Neglect** | After 1303 it was never fully repaired; nobody wanted to pay for another rebuild | Medium | 0.7 |
| **Reuse** | Its stones went into the Citadel of Qaitbay (1477) | The final blow | 0.9 |

An engineering study (Abdelnaby and Elnashai, 2013) simulated how the tower would have behaved in the 1303 earthquake. It is one of the first attempts to explain the collapse with modern earthquake engineering. **Confidence: 0.8.**

### 6.2 The legend of the Byzantine spy
The 10th-century Arab historian **al-Masudi** tells how the Byzantine Empire sent an agent who converted to Islam, won the caliph's trust and convinced him there was treasure under the lighthouse. The digging undermined the foundations, part of it collapsed, and the spy escaped by ship.
🧠 **Analysis:** it is a moralising tale (“greed destroys”). There is no evidence. Also, Wikipedia sets it in the time of Caliph **Abd al-Malik** with reign dates of **705–715**, but those are the dates of his son **al-Walid I**; Abd al-Malik reigned **685–705**. It is a minor error in the source, but it shows that everything must be cross-checked. **Confidence that it is a legend: 0.9.**

### 6.3 The Citadel of Qaitbay
In **1477** the Mamluk sultan **al-Ashraf Qaitbay** built a fortress on the spot to defend Alexandria against the Ottomans. He used **the lighthouse platform and its stones**. Whatever he did not use, and had already fallen into the sea, stayed there. **Thanks to that, we know today where to look.** **Confidence: 0.9.**

### 7.1 The main successes
1. **An amateur found it first**: Kamal Abu el-Saadat, diving on his own in 1961.
2. **The 1994 rescue**: a filmmaker and an archaeologist stopped a breakwater that was burying a wonder of the world.
3. **Cataloguing thousands of blocks** in murky water, by hand, with camera and tape measure.
4. **Photogrammetry from 2013**: when the “expensive” technology (sonar) failed, the team invented its own method.
5. **The 2025 lift**: raising 80-tonne pieces without breaking them, then studying and digitising them.

### 7.2 Failures and mistakes
1. **1968**: exploration stopped because the area became military.
2. **1993**: the concrete breakwater crushed and buried part of the site. It is the worst modern damage it has suffered.
3. **Decades of sewage** discharged into the Eastern Harbour, polluting the water and hampering the work (see section 10).
4. **The underwater museum**: announced in 1996–1998, 2008, 2015 and 2025, and still not built.
5. **Scattered figures**: every source gives a different number of blocks and area (see section 17), a sign that the full data has not yet been published.

**Confidence of the table: 0.8.** Some dates (1994 vs 1995 for the “discovery”) change depending on who tells it: the CNRS says 1995 because that is when the blocks were identified as the lighthouse; others say 1994 because that is when excavation began.

## 8. The PHAROS project and the “digital twin” (2022–2026)

### 8.1 What it is
**PHAROS** is a programme of France's **CNRS**, run on the ground by its permanent unit in Egypt, the **Centre for Alexandrian Studies (CEAlex)**, under the authority of **Egypt's Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities**. It is led by archaeologist and architect **Isabelle Hairy**. It is funded mainly by the **Dassault Systèmes Foundation** (which in 2025 paid for the barge and crane).

**Goal:** not to rebuild the lighthouse in stone but to create a **digital twin**: an exact virtual copy in which hypotheses can be tested (how it was built, how it fell) and which can be “visited”.

### 8.2 How it works, step by step
1. **Photogrammetry:** hundreds of photos of each block are taken from every angle and software (for example Agisoft Photoscan) stitches them into a 3D model.
2. **Virtual library:** every digitised block is stored. Before 2025 there were already **over 100 blocks** digitised underwater.
3. **Virtual anastylosis:** like a jigsaw, the computer is used to test where each piece fitted.
4. **Simulation:** Dassault Systèmes' engineering software (CATIA and SIMULIA) checks whether the proposed structure stands up and how it responds to an **earthquake**.
5. **Ancient sources:** everything is cross-checked with the coins, mosaics and medieval Arab descriptions.

### 8.3 The June 2025 operation
- **22 of the largest blocks** came out of the water with a crane on a barge.
- They included **lintels and jambs** of the monumental doorway (**70–80 t** each), the **threshold**, large **base slabs** (up to **40 t**) and pieces of a **pylon** with a doorway in **Egyptian style but Greek technique**, a monument nobody knew about.
- After being cleaned, photographed and measured, **the pieces were put back underwater** for long-term preservation. Three independent sources confirm it: *Journal du Net* (October 2026), the *New York Times* (February 2026) and the director of CEAlex, Thomas Faucher, speaking to *Ancient History X*. **Confidence: 0.85.**
- In the same *New York Times* report, Isabelle Hairy estimates the project is **still generations from completion**, held back by minimal state funding and **worsening pollution**.

### 8.4 What the model has shown (2026)
| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Height | **103 m**, in three volumes on a plinth |
| Access | Causeway on **16 arches** up to a **13 m** doorway |
| Interior | **67 rooms** on **9 levels**, connected by a ramp |

## 9. Current state of the site (2026)

### 9.1 Where and in what condition
| Aspect | Status |
|---|---|
| Location | At the foot of the **Citadel of Qaitbay**, at the entrance to the Eastern Harbour |
| Depth | **2.6–9 m** (most blocks between 4 and 9 m) |
| Area | Between **1.6 and a little over 3 ha** depending on the source and what counts as the site |
| Pieces | From **3,032 catalogued** (2017) to **over 5,000 recorded** (2025–2026) |
| Share of the lighthouse | About **10%** of the original building, mixed with pieces from other monuments |
| Breakwater | The 1993 concrete blocks are still there, on top of or next to part of the site |
| Water | **Murky**, with organic pollution (see section 10) |
| Erosion | Stone surfaces are worn after centuries underwater |
| Visits | The area can be **dived** with authorisation (the so-called “underwater museum” or archaeological park, which is not actually a building) |

### 9.2 Current threats
1. **Pollution:** sewage, especially from an outfall next to Qaitbay, and fishing-boat activity.
2. **Silting:** the breakwaters change the currents and sand gradually covers pieces.
3. **Sea-level rise and delta subsidence:** Alexandria is one of the Mediterranean cities most exposed to climate change. There are very alarming estimates for 2050, but how reliable they are depends on the scenario. **Confidence: 0.5.**
4. **Erosion and organisms** attaching to the stone (see section 11).
5. **Looting:** one of the reasons behind the 2008 underwater museum project.
6. **Coastal and urban works:** the 1993 breakwater proved that one badly planned project can destroy more in weeks than the sea in centuries.

🧠 **My assessment:** the site is **not going to “disappear”** the way people fear for the Titanic: stone and granite last thousands of years underwater. The real danger is not the sea but **people**: construction, pollution and looting. **Confidence: 0.75.**

## 10. The environment: diving at 2–9 metres in Alexandria

| Parameter | Approximate value | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Depth | 2.6–9 m | A **very shallow** dive: within entry-level recreational limits as far as depth goes |
| Pressure at 9 m | **~1.9 bar** absolute | 1 bar of atmosphere + 0.9 bar of water |
| Light | Sunlight reaches the bottom | At this depth there is photosynthesis: real **seaweed** grows |
| Visibility | **Poor**, often only a few metres | Particles, plankton and pollution. One of the archaeologists' biggest problems |
| Currents and swell | Variable | The coast faces the open sea; with swell the water gets even murkier |
| Eastern Harbour | ~2.5 km², average depth **6.5 m** | A shallow, semi-enclosed basin: the water is renewed slowly |
| Pollution | Moderate to high depending on the season | Outfall next to Qaitbay, fishing boats, tourism. In the 1990s the sewage of a city of millions was discharged into the harbour |
| Traffic | Fishing and tourist boats | A risk to divers without a surface marker buoy |

🧠 **Calculation (as a diver):** at 9 m the absolute pressure is 1 + 9/10 = 1.9 bar. A 12 L cylinder at 200 bar holds ~2,400 L of air; at 1.9 bar, with a relaxed surface consumption of 20 L/min (≈ 38 L/min at that depth), it would last more than 50 minutes even keeping a reserve. **The real limit of the dive is not air but visibility, cold and finding your way among the blocks.** **Confidence: 0.8** (these are generic calculations; every diver breathes differently).

⚠️ **Warning:** this is not a dive guide. Any dive on an archaeological site needs permission from the Egyptian authorities and an authorised centre. **Touching or taking anything is forbidden.**

## 11. Animals and “vegetation” among the stones

<p align="center">
  <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8e/AlexLighthouse04.jpg" width="600" alt="Stones on the site covered in algae">
  <br><sub><i>Stones on the site near the former location of the lighthouse, covered in a layer of algae and organisms. Photo: Roland Unger, CC BY-SA 3.0 (Wikimedia Commons).</i></sub>
</p>

### 11.1 Here there really is vegetation
🧠 **Reasoning:** unlike the Titanic (3,800 m, total darkness), here we are at **less than 10 m**: sunlight gets through and there is **photosynthesis**. That is why the stones are covered in real **algae**. But murky, nutrient-rich water favours “tough” algae and harms the more delicate ones. **Confidence: 0.9.**

### 11.2 What scientific studies of the Eastern Harbour say
There is no fauna survey of the **lighthouse site itself**, but there are several of **hard substrates in the Eastern Harbour** (rocks, blocks and structures), which is the same environment:

| Group | Species recorded | What they are |
|---|---|---|
| **Red algae** | *Jania rubens*, *Corallina*, *Amphiroa*, *Pterocladia* | Algae with a lime skeleton (calcareous) or small bush-like forms |
| **Green algae** | *Ulva* (sea lettuce), *Cladophora*, *Enteromorpha* | Very typical of **nutrient-rich or polluted water** |
| **Brown algae** | *Colpomenia*, *Petalonia* | Small algae shaped like balloons or sheets |
| **Mussels** | ***Brachidontes pharaonis*** | A small **invasive** mussel that came from the Red Sea through the Suez Canal |
| **Polychaete worms** | *Pseudonereis anomala*, *Spirobranchus triqueter* (calcareous tube worm), *Syllis*, *Loimia medusa* | Marine worms; some live inside tubes they build on the stone |
| **Crustaceans** | Amphipods (*Elasmopus pectenicrus*), isopods (*Cirolana bovina*, *Dynamene bidentata*), **acorn barnacles** (*Balanus*) | Small marine “woodlice” and fixed crustaceans |
| **Bryozoans** | Several species | Tiny colonies that form crusts |
| **Limpets** | *Fissurella* | Molluscs with a cone-shaped shell stuck to the rock |

A fixed-site study in the Eastern Harbour (2014–2018) found only **26 species** of hard-bottom fauna: **low diversity**, typical of shallow, murky areas with lots of sediment and human pressure. A winter storm in 2014 reduced the species even further, and the toughest ones won out (such as the tube worm *Spirobranchus triqueter*). **Confidence: 0.75.**

### 11.3 Fish
- Beach-seine fishing in the Eastern Harbour (2009) recorded **46 fish species** from 31 families. The study warns that fishing with very fine-mesh nets in nursery areas endangers the populations.
- Many fish on this coast are **Lessepsian species**, meaning **they arrived from the Red Sea through the Suez Canal** (opened in 1869):
  - **Rabbitfish** (*Siganus rivulatus*): one of the most abundant on the Egyptian coast. It is herbivorous and, where common, can strip rocks bare of algae.
  - **Lionfish** (*Pterois miles*): **venomous** and invasive. Its first confirmed record in Egypt's Mediterranean waters is from **2018**. If you see one while diving, **do not touch it**: its spines give very painful stings.
- ❓ Typical Mediterranean animals of shallow rocks, such as **octopus, sea urchins, sea bream or moray eels**, are **probably** there, but **I have not found a study that records them for this particular site**. **This is a hypothesis. Confidence: 0.5.**

### 11.4 What do living things do to the stones?
- **Calcareous algae, barnacles, tube worms and bryozoans** form a **crust** (*fouling*) on the blocks. On the one hand it **protects** the surface from sand abrasion; on the other it **hides** carvings and inscriptions and has to be cleaned off before photographing.
- Some organisms (boring sponges, some molluscs and worms) can slowly **dig into** limestone (**bioerosion**). Granite resists much better.
**Confidence: 0.65** (this is general knowledge in underwater archaeology; I have not found a bioerosion study specific to this site).

  <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9d/AlexLighthouse03.jpg" width="420" alt="Stone mortar underwater">

| Type of object | Approximate number | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Architectural blocks** (granite and limestone) | 3,000–5,000+ | Many from the lighthouse: lintels, jambs, threshold, slabs, base pieces. The heaviest exceed **40 t**, and the doorway pieces reach **70–80 t** |

Some researchers think part of them formed a medieval defensive **barrier** against enemy ships. Identifying each block is **detective work**: measurements, material, clamp marks and comparison with ancient coins and texts. **Confidence: 0.7.**

### 13.1 What happens to a stone when it comes out of the sea?
🧠 A stone that has spent centuries in the sea is **soaked with salt**. If it dries out suddenly, the salt **crystallises inside the pores** and breaks it apart from within (the stone flakes). That is why it has to be **desalinated**: kept for months or years in fresh water that is changed regularly until the salt slowly leaves. It is expensive, slow and needs space. This is why **taking something out of the water is not “saving” it** unless there is a conservation plan behind it. **Confidence: 0.85.**

## 14. Touch the site? Raise objects? The big debate

### 14.1 Rebuild the lighthouse for real?
Modern replicas have been proposed since **1978**, and in **2015** the government and the Alexandria Governorate even floated a **skyscraper** on the site as part of the harbour's regeneration. Many experts and residents criticised it.

| ✅ For | ❌ Against |
|---|---|
| It would attract tourism and create jobs | A replica is a new building, not the Pharos |
| It would restore a symbol of the city | The Citadel of Qaitbay (15th century) is heritage too and sits right on top |
| | Construction could damage the underwater remains (as in 1993) |
| | Nobody knows exactly what it looked like: half the building would be “invented” |

🧠 **Conclusion:** the **digital twin** gives almost every advantage of a rebuild (seeing the lighthouse, understanding it, visiting it) **without touching a single stone**. **Confidence: 0.8.**

### 14.2 Take the pieces out of the water?

| ✅ Arguments for raising them | ❌ Arguments against |
|---|---|
| In the water they are exposed to **pollution, silt and looting** | **UNESCO** recommends **in situ conservation** as the first option |
| Out of the water they can be **studied** closely: inscriptions, tool marks, clamps | Removing a piece **decontextualises** it: information about where and how it lay is lost |
| People can see them without diving: **educational value** | **Desalinating** and conserving 40–80-tonne pieces is very expensive and slow |
| Some pieces could go to museums around the world | The current seabed is almost a **natural museum**: divers see the site as it really is |
| | The stones **will not dissolve** in the water (unlike the Titanic's iron) |
| | Egypt has **thousands** of pieces: there is neither space nor money for all of them |

### 14.3 The approach being used: “raise, study and return”
The **2025 lift** shows a middle way:
1. Only **key pieces** that cannot be studied properly underwater are raised.
2. They are **cleaned, measured and digitised** in 3D.
3. **They are returned to the seabed** (confirmed by CEAlex and the *New York Times*), where they are better protected than on land without a conservation plan.
4. Everything else is **digitised without being moved**, using underwater photogrammetry.

### 14.4 My reasoned conclusion
1. **Rebuilding the lighthouse in stone:** no. **Confidence: 0.8.**
2. **Leaving most blocks where they are** and documenting them in 3D: yes. **Confidence: 0.85.**
3. **Raising specific pieces**, only for scientific purposes and with a plan to conserve them or return them to the water: yes, as in 2025. **Confidence: 0.75.**
4. **Statues and unique pieces** at risk of looting or breaking: this is where the real debate is. Without surveillance in the water, a museum may be the better option. **Confidence: 0.55** (it is an ethical and practical decision; there is no objective answer).
5. **The most urgent thing is not raising anything:** it is **stopping the pollution** of the Eastern Harbour and **not repeating the 1993 breakwater mistake**. **Confidence: 0.8.**

## 18. Limitations of the study

1. **I have not dived the site:** everything comes from published sources.
2. **The full PHAROS data is not public:** there are only press releases, articles and documentaries. The final scientific publications are still missing.
3. **The weight of the 2025 blocks** varies: CNRS gives 70–80 t for the doorway, while another source mentions 40–50 t blocks (probably the slabs).
4. **Fauna and flora:** there is no biological inventory of **the lighthouse site itself**; I use studies of the Eastern Harbour, which is the same environment but not exactly the same spot. The presence of octopus, urchins or moray eels is a **hypothesis**.
5. **Ancient sources** (Strabo, Pliny, Lucian, Arab authors) were written centuries after the construction or with imprecise measurements.
6. **Figures for pieces, area and depth** do not match between sources (see 17.2).
7. **The ethical debate** in section 14 is a reasoned opinion, not a fact.
8. **The legal situation and the projects** (underwater museum) may have changed after November 2026.
